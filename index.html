    FEE MATH

    What a sale actually leaves you

    The arbitrage engine never compares raw price gaps. Every trade is netted of the selling marketplace's commission, payment fee and postage before it counts as an opportunity. That's what kills most of the "deals" a naive price diff finds.

    This receipt runs the engine's Cardmarket defaults: 5% commission, 3% payment fee and €1.50 postage. Drag the price down and watch the fixed postage eat a cheap card.

    Three problems worth the write-up

    Straight out of the project's lessons log. Every number below is measured, and most of them came from a bug I had to find first.

    CASE 01 · API QUOTA BUDGET

    Running a price app on a thousand calls a month

    The main catalogue API's free tier is 1,000 calls a month. The monthly cap binds before the daily one, so the real sustained budget is about 33 calls a day. That one number sets the 15-minute cache, the hourly alert check, the lazy-loaded tabs and the rate limiter.

    Card-number and set-code searches now run against a local catalogue of 2,495 cards, imported from image filenames at zero API cost, so a typo can't drain the month. When I needed to know whether a batch of ids costs one call or twenty, I measured it with three balance readings instead of guessing.

    The same readings turned up 880 of 1,000 calls already spent. A background sweep had been running at six times its default budget and stopped itself quietly at its safety floor. Nothing errored and nothing logged. It showed up only in the balance.

    CASE 02 · PRICE-DATA INTEGRITY

    The 1,594% return that wasn't there

    A Quick Scan on Dracule Mihawk OP01-070 said: buy raw at $1.06, grade it, sell the PSA 10 at $949.95, a 1594.83% return. Re-running with grading switched off returned zero opportunities. The grading path was producing every result the feature had, and all of them were false.

    The comp filter could prove a premium marker was present in a listing title but never that it was absent, so every premium printing sharing the card number counted as a comp. Adding keywords made it worse, because tournament-prize cards carry no marker at all. The fix classifies which printing a title names in both directions, then makes the comp pool agree with itself or return nothing.

    All three flagged cards now refuse, each for a different reason (spread 10.0×, 2 comps, spread 4.7×). 7 of 10 surveyed cards still produce a coherent pool, clustering at 1.1–2.0× spread, which is where the 4× threshold comes from.

    CASE 03 · CROSS-MARKET MATCHING

    Every printing, its own Cardmarket page

    A Parallel and its base card share a card number but not a price, so every printing needs its own link. The first importer scored 152 of 156 on the dev database and 2,316 of 6,950 in production. Dev held a single set, the one set where the rule's assumption happened to hold.

    That assumption was that a card number's prefix names its set. It doesn't: premium boosters file cards under the original set's number, promos live in four differently coded expansions, and two catalogues spell the same release differently. Blueprints are now indexed by their own collector prefix across all 99 expansions, and the set name scores the candidates instead of gating one, with zero unresolvable ties.

    By the evening it was 5,688 of 6,950 linked, 85% of every printing that has a card number. What's left is left on purpose, because a card we can't place keeps a search link rather than a wrong one.

    LESSON LOGGEDMeasure a catalogue rule on the catalogue, not on the dev database.
